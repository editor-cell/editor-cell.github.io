    How much water do data centres use?

    The answer has two parts: the water evaporated to cool the servers, and the water used to make their electricity.

    The digital world and artificial intelligence possess an invisible thirst. Data centres are massive electricity users; the IEA estimates they consumed about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024 (1.5 percent of global electricity), a figure expected to more than double by 2030 as AI grows.

    The digital world and artificial intelligence possess an invisible thirst. Data centres are massive electricity users; the IEA estimates they consumed about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024 (1.5 percent of global electricity), a figure expected to more than double by 2030 as AI grows. In many large facilities, the immense heat from this electricity is removed by evaporating water in cooling towers, and that evaporated water never returns to the pipe. When the water used by thermal power plants to generate that electricity is added, the toll grows heavier. Researchers estimated that training the GPT-3 model in Microsoft's American data centres could have evaporated about 700,000 litres of clean fresh water on site alone. With every answer AI generates, it takes a sip of water from nature.

    In the climate crisis, national greenhouse gas inventories kept by governments serve as official, yet slow-moving ledgers of what is burned and released. Today, however, satellites in orbit measure methane plumes and changes in carbon dioxide, opening a completely independent set of books. Scientists use this data to question the discrepancies between official paper figures and what the atmosphere shows – such as a missing landfill or a revised emission factor. As synthesis reports by the IPCC and NASA unequivocally state, warming is human-driven. Reconciling orbital books with state ledgers isn't about declaring a winner; it increases the resolution on exactly which sectors need a closer audit to save the planet.

An official document released by the state is only as public as the borders of its language. From school guidance to census notes, any record published in a language not spoken in a city's homes is formally public but practically closed. UNESCO highlights this gap by noting that a large share of learners are not taught in a language they understand well. Statistical databases suffer from the same bottleneck; for instance, while the UN Sustainable Development Goals database tries to keep indicators in more than one UN language, the depth of translation remains uneven. The true width of a public claim is measured by the number of languages in which it can be opened and read.

  1. After ten million

    A megacity used to be an exception.

    Politics

    1 min read

    Once, "megacities" with populations over ten million were rare exceptions on the map; today, according to UN data, they form a class large enough to be compared with one another. Yet looking at glittering skylines or population tables tells us nothing about how those residents are actually housed. Data from institutions like the World Bank show how far official figures can disagree on informal settlements; a city may officially boom while the count of homes with actual services barely moves. What truly differentiates megacities is not the height of their towers, but whether their census and utility planning can keep pace with the people they encompass.

  2. How grain actually moves

    Wheat, maize, and rice cross borders as balance sheets before they cross them as ships.

    Economy

    1 min read

    Long before wheat, maize, and rice cross oceans in ships, they cross borders as contracts and balance sheets in offices. Balance sheets published by agencies like the FAO (detailing production, trade and year-end stocks) reveal how concentrated the grain trade is. A small group of exporters still supplies a large share of traded wheat, and as AMIS data show, when a trade corridor slows, the paper trail makes clear which importing country will see the delay become a domestic question. However, reading these balance sheets accurately means noticing the gaps: sacks that never reach customs, cargo refused for quality, or harvest lost before the farm gate never make it into the official record.

  3. The register of trials

    A study that never enters a public register is harder to find, and harder to count.

    Health

    1 min read

    In the medical world, a clinical trial is a plan long before it is a result. Such plans – detailing who is enrolled and what is measured – can be filed in public registries before the first participant is even seen, and the WHO maintains a portal that searches all the registries meeting its standard in one place. The massive registry run by the US National Library of Medicine is the largest of these, but it is just one book, not the entire shelf. Medical journal editors treat trial registration not merely as a courtesy, but as a foundational piece of the scientific record. A trial that is never recorded in a public register, where one cannot walk back to see what initial question was asked, is far more likely to vanish in the dark.

    Why are rivers running dry?

    Some of the world's great rivers no longer reach the sea for part of the year.

    Because its water is divided among seven US states and Mexico and nearly all of it is taken for farms and cities, the Colorado River has failed to reach the sea for most years since the 1960s. Similarly, massive diversions of the Amu Darya and Syr…

    Because its water is divided among seven US states and Mexico and nearly all of it is taken for farms and cities, the Colorado River has failed to reach the sea for most years since the 1960s. Similarly, massive diversions of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers for cotton irrigation shrank the once-mighty Aral Sea to a mere fraction of its size. The true culprit is human choice rather than nature. According to the FAO, agriculture accounts for about 70 percent of fresh water taken globally. Furthermore, a global mapping found that only 37 percent of rivers longer than 1,000 kilometres still flow freely along their whole length. While a warming climate makes the problem worse, these rivers often run dry not from a lack of rainfall, but because of decisions made on paper about how much water to take.

    How do city lights affect sleep?

    Most people now live under a night sky that never fully darkens.

    A 2016 mapping found that over 80 percent of the world's population – and over 99 percent in the US and Europe – live under light-polluted skies, hiding the Milky Way from a third of humanity. From volunteers' reports between…

    A 2016 mapping found that over 80 percent of the world's population – and over 99 percent in the US and Europe – live under light-polluted skies, hiding the Milky Way from a third of humanity. From volunteers' reports between 2011 and 2022, Kyba and colleagues calculated that the sky is brightening by about 9.6 percent a year. Our bodies read light as a "time signal." Evening light, especially blue light, delays the release of melatonin, the hormone preparing us for sleep, and the white LEDs now common in street lighting emit a large share of their light in that blue range. A study of nearly 16,000 Americans found that people in brighter areas tended to go to bed later, sleep less, and feel less satisfied with their rest. The link is an association, not proof; the light that reaches a bedroom also depends on curtains and screens. Cities that want darker nights have simple tools: downward-pointing shielded lamps, warmer hues, and dimming in the late hours.
