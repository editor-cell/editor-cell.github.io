Migration routes do not just rewrite demographics; they change the chemistry of the ballot box. But exactly how? The question usually gets lost in the noise of political polling; we find the answer instead in those quiet, experimental periods when the state, not the migrants, decided where people would settle. Denmark's policy from 1986 to 1998 holds up a clear mirror. When refugees were placed in municipalities with little regard for local politics, studies found that in most municipalities, receiving more refugees raised support for anti-immigration parties. In the largest and most urban municipalities, however, the effect went the other way. A similar wind blew through Austria: where more immigrants lived, worries about jobs and about the quality of neighbourhoods channelled votes to the far-right Freedom Party, especially when the newcomers had little formal education. The core of the issue, however, lies in the depth of contact. Twice a year, in the dead of night, when we move our clocks an hour forward or back, we are not really playing with time but with an illusion of our own invention. Most governments now agree that this artificial pendulum swing has outlived its usefulness. But the real quarrel breaks out when the question is asked: "Which hour will we keep?" When the European Union put this rule, in place since 2001, to a public consultation in 2018, an overwhelming 84 percent of the 4.6 million respondents wanted the clock changes to stop. Although the European Parliament voted in 2019 to end the practice by 2021, the political chessboard among member states remains deadlocked. Imagine it: if one country chooses summer daylight and its neighbour opts for winter mornings, the two could end up an hour apart, and the map of Europe could turn into a patchwork of time zones. Russia acted as a laboratory for this dilemma. It moved the clocks forward in 2011 and left them there; but endless summer time did not last, and a law passed in July 2014 set the clocks back an hour for good, this time to permanent winter time. Most people now try to sleep under a sky that never truly darkens and whose stars are fading. A 2016 mapping found that over 80 percent of the world's population – and over 99 percent in the US and Europe – live under light-polluted skies, hiding the Milky Way from a third of humanity. From volunteers' reports between 2011 and 2022, Kyba and colleagues calculated that the sky is brightening by about 9.6 percent a year. Our bodies read light as a "time signal." Evening light, especially blue light, delays the release of melatonin, the hormone preparing us for sleep, and the white LEDs now common in street lighting emit a large share of their light in that blue range. A study of nearly 16,000 Americans found that people in brighter areas tended to go to bed later, sleep less, and feel less satisfied with their rest. The link is an association, not proof; the light that reaches a bedroom also depends on curtains and screens. Cities that want darker nights have simple tools: downward-pointing shielded lamps, warmer hues, and dimming in the late hours.

The bill for an earthquake isn't settled when the images of rubble fade from television screens; on the contrary, the real damage in the economy's capillaries begins that very day. The cost of collapsed bridges, pulverised factories and ruined ports – what we call "damage" – is merely the disaster's first face, counted only once. What remains invisible, stretching painfully over time, is the "loss of output": the value that city would have created over the years had it remained standing. Viewed from a distance, national economies are surprisingly resilient. Studies show that, on average, even very large disasters do not leave a lasting mark on national output, unless they are followed by radical political change such as a revolution. National averages, however, are masters at masking quiet, local tragedies. The January 1995 earthquake that struck Kobe, the pride of Japan, didn't just claim more than 6,400 lives; it wrecked much of what was then one of the world's busiest container ports. When it comes to bread on the table, what the grain has kept matters more than whether bread is eaten at all. The WHO notes that adults living with diabetes rose from about 200 million in 1990 to about 830 million in 2022; most have type 2 diabetes, closely linked to excess weight and low activity. Research separates whole grain, which keeps the bran and the germ, from refined grain, which is mostly starch. People eating about three servings of whole grains a day had roughly a third lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes than those eating almost none, while refined grains showed no such association. Analyses following nearly 195,000 adults for decades reached similar results for whole-grain foods like dark bread, oatmeal and cereals. The usual explanation lies in fibre and structure: starch from white flour raises blood sugar fast, while bran slows digestion and prolongs fullness. These studies observe what people eat rather than assign it, and people who choose whole grains may differ in other ways. A brown colour, too, is not proof of whole grain, which makes label-reading essential.

Does consuming more equate to living better? In 1974, Richard Easterlin noted a puzzle: within a country, richer people were happier, yet average happiness barely moved as countries grew wealthier – and a long argument about the meaning of growth began. However, research based on far larger surveys argues that doubling an income brings a similar gain whether one starts poor or rich, meaning each additional dollar buys less happiness. Do the gains ever stop? A study based on about 450,000 survey responses from American residents found that while overall life evaluation rose with income, day-to-day emotional improvement plateaued at about 75,000 dollars a year. In 2023, the two sides of the debate analysed the data together and found that for most people, happiness keeps rising with income; the plateau applies only to the least happy fifth of people, beyond about 100,000 dollars. Furthermore, how money is spent matters: people spending money on others, as gifts or donations, reported greater happiness than those spending it on themselves.

The sea is never still; tides and winds move its surface daily. The oldest tool, the tide gauge, records water height against land, with the Permanent Service for Mean Sea Level collecting such records since 1933. Yet land moves too, with coastal cities often sinking as groundwater is pumped out, so modern gauges are paired with satellite positioning receivers that measure the movement of the land itself. Since 1992, satellites using radar altimeters have also measured the open ocean directly by timing radar pulses. Combined, these records reveal a stark truth: according to the IPCC, global mean sea level rose by about 20 centimetres between 1901 and 2018, with the rate accelerating from 1.9 millimetres a year (1971-2006) to 3.7 millimetres (2006-2018). As warming water expands and melting glaciers add water to the oceans, the fate of coasts is dictated by this relentless, millimetre-by-millimetre advance.

Some of the world's most magnificent rivers no longer reach the sea. Because its water is divided among seven US states and Mexico and nearly all of it is taken for farms and cities, the Colorado River has failed to reach the sea for most years since the 1960s. Similarly, massive diversions of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers for cotton irrigation shrank the once-mighty Aral Sea to a mere fraction of its size. The true culprit is human choice rather than nature. According to the FAO, agriculture accounts for about 70 percent of fresh water taken globally. Furthermore, a global mapping found that only 37 percent of rivers longer than 1,000 kilometres still flow freely along their whole length. While a warming climate makes the problem worse, these rivers often run dry not from a lack of rainfall, but because of decisions made on paper about how much water to take.

The carbon books, reconciled

National inventories and satellite measurements are being read against each other – not as a headline, but as an accounting problem.

In the climate crisis, national greenhouse gas inventories kept by governments serve as official, yet slow-moving ledgers of what is burned and released. Today, however, satellites in orbit measure methane plumes and changes in carbon dioxide, opening a completely independent set of books. Scientists use this data to…

In the climate crisis, national greenhouse gas inventories kept by governments serve as official, yet slow-moving ledgers of what is burned and released. Today, however, satellites in orbit measure methane plumes and changes in carbon dioxide, opening a completely independent set of books. Scientists use this data to question the discrepancies between official paper figures and what the atmosphere shows – such as a missing landfill or a revised emission factor. As synthesis reports by the IPCC and NASA unequivocally state, warming is human-driven. Reconciling orbital books with state ledgers isn't about declaring a winner; it increases the resolution on exactly which sectors need a closer audit to save the planet.

    After ten million

    A megacity used to be an exception.

    Politics

    1 min read · 2 sources

    Once, "megacities" with populations over ten million were rare exceptions on the map; today, according to UN data, they form a class large enough to be compared with one another. Yet looking at glittering skylines or population tables tells us nothing about how those residents are actually housed. Data from institutions like the World Bank show how far official figures can disagree on informal settlements; a city may officially boom while the count of homes with actual services barely moves. What truly differentiates megacities is not the height of their towers, but whether their census and utility planning can keep pace with the people they encompass.

  2. 2

    How grain actually moves

    Wheat, maize, and rice cross borders as balance sheets before they cross them as ships.

    Economy

    1 min read · 2 sources

    Long before wheat, maize, and rice cross oceans in ships, they cross borders as contracts and balance sheets in offices. Balance sheets published by agencies like the FAO (detailing production, trade and year-end stocks) reveal how concentrated the grain trade is. A small group of exporters still supplies a large share of traded wheat, and as AMIS data show, when a trade corridor slows, the paper trail makes clear which importing country will see the delay become a domestic question. However, reading these balance sheets accurately means noticing the gaps: sacks that never reach customs, cargo refused for quality, or harvest lost before the farm gate never make it into the official record.

  3. 3

    The register of trials

    A study that never enters a public register is harder to find, and harder to count.

    Health

    1 min read · 3 sources

    In the medical world, a clinical trial is a plan long before it is a result. Such plans – detailing who is enrolled and what is measured – can be filed in public registries before the first participant is even seen, and the WHO maintains a portal that searches all the registries meeting its standard in one place. The massive registry run by the US National Library of Medicine is the largest of these, but it is just one book, not the entire shelf. Medical journal editors treat trial registration not merely as a courtesy, but as a foundational piece of the scientific record. A trial that is never recorded in a public register, where one cannot walk back to see what initial question was asked, is far more likely to vanish in the dark.
